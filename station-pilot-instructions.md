# Station Pilot Instructions, ATP Event, September 17, 2026

## What your session actually is

Your station is a guided discussion, not a talk. You work from a list of roughly eight questions that you put to your station, and the room answers. You won't get to all of them, and that's fine. Your job is to keep the discussion moving and to make sure it lands on the recording.

## Before the Event

### 1. Your dry run with Fleet Command

Nobody flies without a rehearsal. John Trainor is Fleet Command for this event. He's hosting and recording every station's Teams meeting.

**Your dry run gets booked live during the prep meeting on Monday, September 14.** Bring your calendar. Everyone walks out of that meeting with a 15-minute slot on the books, held between the 14th and the 16th. Don't wait to be chased.

The dry run uses your actual meeting link and the exact setup you'll use on the day. You'll confirm you get in with no lobby or waiting room, confirm your audio, confirm noise suppression is off, and confirm recording and transcription are capturing correctly. You'll also walk a few steps away from the laptop and talk like an attendee would, because that's the audio that actually matters. If it works in the test, it works on the day. If anything's off, this is when to fix it.

### 2. Your setup

- Install the Teams desktop app and sign in ahead of the dry run.
- **Your laptop's built-in mic, and nothing else.** No lapel mics, no wireless mics, no phone acting as a second microphone. The format is you putting questions to a room and the room answering, and every extra audio source adds one more thing to manage and one more way to lose the session. One laptop, one audio source.
- **Turn Teams noise suppression off.** Not Auto, off. Settings > Devices > Noise suppression > Off. This is the single most important setting on your machine. Noise suppression is built to isolate one person at a desk, which means it will strip out exactly the room discussion we're there to capture. Set it during the dry run and re-check it on the day.
- Position the laptop for the room, not for you. It doesn't need to present anything, so put it wherever it has the best chance of hearing the discussion.
- **Bring your own laptop, fully charged, with its power cord.** Yours, specifically, so you know the password and can change your own settings. Charge it before you arrive rather than counting on the venue, bring the cord anyway, and keep it plugged in during your session if you can reach an outlet. Then: don't let it sleep, and don't let it lock. A machine that goes to sleep stops the meeting, and a locked machine that nobody can get back into is the most likely way a station loses its transcript entirely.
- Stay within about a meter of the laptop whenever you speak.

### 3. Your questions

Tom Lasswell is drafting the question list for each station. Once you have it, it's yours: read it, cut anything you wouldn't actually ask, reword what you want, and send the result back. Every question comes with a *Run it* line, which is the mechanic for getting the whole room into it (see "Running a room of twenty-five" below). Those are suggestions, not script. A few ground rules that make the whole event work:

- **Roughly eight questions**, sized to what you can genuinely get through. Not a long menu you'll only scratch. If the list is longer than your session, cut it.
- **Get your final version to John no later than Tuesday, September 15.** Wednesday is workable, Thursday morning is the outer edge. Late is survivable. Missing is not.
- **Don't radically rewrite the list at the last minute.** Trimming a question or rewording one is fine, any time. Swapping the whole set on the day is not.
- **Stay reasonably close to your list in the room.** Following the discussion where it goes is good facilitation, and it's fine as long as the audio is clean. The catch is that nobody knows whether the audio was clean until after the session is over, so the questions are the one thing we can count on.

That last point is the real reason this matters. Your question list, with likely answers pre-filled against it, is the backup plan. If your recording or transcript has any problem on the day, that list is what represents your station in the closeout session. The more accurate it is, the better your station comes through even in a worst case.

### 4. Running a room of twenty-five

An open question put to twenty-five people gets answered by the same three. The whole closeout is built from what the other twenty-two say, so the questions are written to pull them in. The mechanics:

- **Open with the ground rule, out loud.** This is recorded, the transcript goes public in this repo, and nobody needs to name their company. Talk about the pattern, not the incident. People say far more once they know the rules than when they're guessing at them.
- **Run Q1 as a round.** Everyone answers in one breath: a number, a word, a tool name. That puts ten voices on the recording in the first three minutes, and it trains the room to talk toward the laptop before anything important gets said.
- **Poll first, then story.** Every question starts with hands up or a number round, so everyone is in before anyone tells a story. Then take two or three stories, never more. Nobody holds the room for more than a couple of minutes.
- **Count out loud.** "That's eleven hands for nobody owns it." A count in your voice, close to the mic, is a number the closeout can use. Hands in the air are invisible to a transcript.
- **Split the room on purpose.** Where a question has two honest sides, ask for hands on each and take one story from each side. Disagreement is the most valuable thing a station can send to the closeout, and it does not happen by itself.
- **Ask the other side.** Vendors and consultants are in your room. When a question is about a buyer's contract or a buyer's data, turn to them: what do their customers get wrong?
- **Ask the closer no matter what.** When the five-minute warning comes, go straight to Q8 wherever you are. It's the one question the closeout can compare across all five stations.

## Day of the Event

- **Arrive at 5:15 PM.** Earlier than you'd think necessary, for two reasons: Roswell traffic on a Thursday evening, and the fact that we want your laptop set up, its settings confirmed, and a live test meeting run on the actual venue Wi-Fi before the room fills up.
- Join your Teams meeting a few minutes early.
- Open with your name, station, and topic spoken out loud. This becomes a marker in the transcript.
- **Your job is getting the room's answers onto the recording.** You're asking the questions, so your voice is the one thing that was never at risk. Everything of value in your session comes out of the room, several feet from a laptop that's straining to hear it. Two things to do about that, both of them constantly:
  - **Tell the room, out loud and more than once, to speak toward the laptop.** Say why: it's how their contribution gets memorialized, and it's the only reason anything they say reaches the closeout session. People cooperate readily once they know a machine is listening on their behalf, but they forget within a couple of minutes, so keep saying it.
  - **Echo the good answers back.** When somebody says something worth keeping, repeat or summarize it before you move on. "So what I'm hearing is the vendor changed the pricing model mid-contract." It's good facilitation anyway, and it means the point survives in your voice, close to the mic, even if their version of it didn't reach the laptop at all.
- **You'll have a Flight Engineer.** Every station gets one: a volunteer whose only job is to watch the machine so you don't have to. They confirm recording is running, that the live transcript is actually building, and that nothing has gone to sleep, locked, or dropped off the network. If you brought your own backup recorder, they also carry it around the room (see below). Tell them at the start that power, sleep, and lock are what you want watched, and then go run your room.
- When you're done, **explicitly end the meeting.** Don't just close your laptop, hit Leave, or walk away. Ending the meeting is what tells Teams to finalize the session and start generating the transcript. Leaving it does not.

### A second capture, on a completely different device

Plans stop working. Everything that goes right after that goes right because somebody had a second way through. This is ours.

If you already run your own recorder or transcription service and trust it, please use it. Three conditions, and the first one is the one people get wrong:

- **It has to be a completely different device.** A transcription service running on the same laptop that's running Teams is not a backup. It's a second app listening to the same microphone in the same spot, so every way the primary capture can fail, it fails too: the laptop sleeps, the laptop locks, the room is too far away, the battery dies. Worse, the two can fight over microphone access and take out the primary. A separate phone or handheld recorder is the whole point.
- **Your Flight Engineer carries it around the room.** This is the part worth planning for. The laptop is fixed in place and straining to hear; a handheld device walked toward whoever's talking hears exactly what the laptop can't. That's the one thing a second capture does genuinely better than the primary, and it's most of the reason to bother. Hand it to your Flight Engineer and let them work the room.
- **You have to be able to get it to John quickly and reliably.** Email it to **john@johntrainor.com as soon as your session ends**, not later that evening and not the next morning. There are only a few minutes between your station wrapping and the closeout session. If your service holds the file behind a login, a manual export step, or a processing queue you can't predict, it isn't usable as backup, and you're better off skipping it than counting on it.
