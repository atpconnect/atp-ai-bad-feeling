# Ice Planet: Question List and Seeded Answers

**Theme:** Use cases, costs, and scope creep
**Station pilot:** Mat Matthews, CIO, Fortrex Solutions
**Status:** draft, 2026-09-11

The shield doors are closed. Everyone's dug in at the outpost, waiting on evacuation orders that haven't come yet. This station is about the AI use cases stuck in exactly that position: not cancelled, not shipped, just waiting on a decision nobody has made, while the cost meter keeps running and the original ask quietly grows into something nobody actually approved. Roughly eight questions, more than we will get through, which is the point. Each question has a *Run it* line: the mechanic for getting twenty-five people into it rather than three. The seeded answers below are Fleet Command's pre-written guesses at what this room is likely to say. They exist only as the fallback if this station's recording fails. Nobody has said any of this yet.

## Q1. How many AI use cases has your company started, and how many are actually in production today?

*Run it:* Round the room, two numbers each. Keep a running total and say it out loud at the end for the recording.

*Seeded likely answer:* Expect double digits started against low single digits live. That ratio is the whole story of this station.

## Q2. What is the single most common reason a use case stalls before it ships?

*Run it:* Poll it: budget, no owner, data, legal, or nobody ever decided. Hands for each, count out loud. Then stories from the biggest pile.

*Seeded likely answer:* No named owner with budget authority once the pilot ends. It stalls in the handoff between the team that built it and the team that would have to run it.

## Q3. What does a stalled project cost you every month it sits there, uncancelled?

*Run it:* Hands up: who has one sitting there right now. Then: what is still being paid for, and who is still nominally on it?

*Seeded likely answer:* Cloud spend that never got turned off, the attention of people still nominally assigned to it, and the credibility of whatever gets proposed next. None of it shows up as a line item anyone actually reviews.

## Q4. How much did the scope grow between the day a project was approved and where it stands today, and who added what?

*Run it:* Hands up: who has a project that picked up a second business unit or an executive's feature. Then: who added it, and did the budget follow?

*Seeded likely answer:* Substantially, and rarely in one direction. A single workflow picks up a compliance review, a second business unit, and an executive's pet feature before anyone goes back and re-scopes the budget.

## Q5. Have you ever killed an AI project outright? What did that actually take?

*Run it:* Hands up. Then: was it a decision on the merits, or a reorg?

*Seeded likely answer:* Rare, and when it happened it usually took a reorg or a budget cut, not a decision anyone made on the project's own merits.

## Q6. What did the bill look like against the pilot estimate once real users hit it: tokens, seats, the people running it?

*Run it:* Round the room: a multiple, not a percentage. Then: which line was the surprise?

*Seeded likely answer:* Multiples, not percentages. The pilot cost was the model calls; production cost was the people reviewing output, the seats nobody counted, and usage that grew with success. The surprise was rarely the token price itself.

## Q7. Which use case did ship and stick, and what was different about it? Has anyone measured whether it paid off?

*Run it:* Hands up: who has one that stuck. Two stories. Then: how do you know it paid off? Expect disagreement about what counts.

*Seeded likely answer:* The one with a narrow, boring workflow, an owner in the business, and a number it was measured against before it started. Few measured after. Most call it a win because it is still running.

## Q8. What would have to be true for the thing sitting in your backlog right now to ship next quarter?

*Run it:* The closer. Ask it at the five-minute warning no matter where you are. Round the room, one thing each.

*Seeded likely answer:* A named production owner, a support model, and one executive willing to accept the risk in writing. Usually just those three things, not a technical fix.
